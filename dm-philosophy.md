# DM Philosophy

The goal of prep is to narrow the improvisation surface to a space you can handle. You are not an improv expert. Don't try to be. Do the creative work before the session so that at the table you're reacting to pre-loaded material, not generating from nothing.

BLeeM's approach distilled: load every scene with specific wants and specific contradictions before play starts. Know what each NPC wants when they walk in. Know what would happen if the players did nothing. Have your if/then decisions made in advance. The rest is reaction.

---

## Loaded Scenes

Every scene should have a charge when the party walks in. Something is already in motion — an NPC wants something, a clock is ticking, the environment is doing something. Players don't generate the tension; they respond to it.

A loaded scene has three things:
- **Who wants what** — at least one NPC or location has a specific want right now, in this scene
- **What's already ticking** — at least one clock is advancing whether or not the players act
- **What happens if they do nothing** — you know this answer before you open the session

"Ariel's island" is a location. "Ariel wants Coriac to hear her unfinished sentence before the party leaves" is a loaded scene element.

---

## NPC Wants

Characterization is not a session want. "Shalindra is manipulative" is characterization. "Shalindra wants to know if the soul forge has been used, and won't reveal that she wants to know" is a session want.

For every NPC likely to appear, know three things:
- **Want tonight** — specific, active, present-tense. What do they want from this scene?
- **Fear tonight** — what outcome costs them something here?
- **Tell** — how do you know when they're getting what they want or losing ground?

Knowing these three things means you know how the NPC behaves in any moment. You don't script their dialogue — you know their want, and they pursue it.

---

## Clocks

Clocks are things that advance whether or not the players act. They create the sense that the world moves on its own.

A clock has:
- **Name** — what is ticking
- **Advance trigger** — what causes a tick (usually "time passes" or "specific action taken / not taken")
- **Stages** — usually 3–4. Starts at stage 1 when you create it.
- **Max outcome** — what happens when the clock fills. This should be something you're willing to let happen, because it will.

The max outcome is irrevocable. If you write "at max, the captain is gone," then when the clock fills, the captain is gone. Don't create clocks you'll fudge. That kills the mechanic.

Clocks make the world feel consequential without requiring you to improvise consequence mid-session.

---

## Trigger Table

Pre-load your if/then decisions. When the party does X, Y happens. Having 8–10 of these ready before a session means you're never generating from nothing — you're choosing from prepared responses, or riffing off one.

Good triggers are:
- Specific to this scene, not generic
- Consequence-forward — Y changes something in the world or a relationship
- Honest — if you wrote "if they find the hat, he went somewhere willingly," then when they find the hat, he went somewhere willingly

Triggers are not scripts. They're decisions you've already made, waiting for the right moment.

## Challenges — Stage Them, Don't Solve Them

A challenge is not a lock you're hiding the one key to. Your job is to build the situation, not the solution. This is the Brennan Lee Mulligan / Matt Mercer principle: the DM creates the puzzle; the players solve it. You are not allowed to have decided the "right" way through, because deciding it makes every other approach feel wrong, and the table goes linear and quiet while they try to read your mind.

Prep a challenge by setting three things and nothing more:

- **The stage** — the obstacle, the materials at hand, what it wants, what's at stake. Make it concrete and physical enough that players can *do* things to it.
- **The success trigger** — what state, once reached, means they're through. Not *how* they reach it — just what "solved" looks like.
- **The fail / force trigger** — what happens if they try to break it, take from it, or barge past. This is usually where the consequence lives.

Then leave the middle empty on purpose. Whatever the players try that satisfies the success trigger *works* — especially the approach you didn't see coming. If you find yourself saying "no, that's not how it's meant to be done," you prepped a solution instead of a challenge. Back up: is the success condition met? Then it worked.

A good challenge has many doors and you haven't picked one. You hold the win and the fail; the players supply the path between them.

## Creative Pain

Choices cost. The consequence ledger records the cost. The ripple is the world's response.

This isn't punishment — it's what makes choices feel real. A choice without a cost is a preference, not a decision. Grimvald's Suggestion on the captain was a choice. The captain's autonomy was the cost. Whatever happens when he learns what was done to him is the ripple.

Your job is to hold the cost clearly and let the ripple happen. Don't soften it.

## The Environment Is a Character

Locations have wants, turns, escalation triggers. Ariel's island isn't a backdrop — it's an entity with a relationship to the party. They landed on it uninvited, mid-fight. What does the island want from this interaction?

Before any major location scene, ask:
- What does this place want from the players in this scene?
- What happens if the players ignore the environment and only interact with each other?
- What's the escalation if they damage it, disrespect it, or stay too long?

The location schema in this repo carries these fields. Use them.

## How This Changes Prep

Instead of plotting what might happen, you prepare material: NPC wants, clocks, trigger table entries. Play is the party interacting with that material.

You're a reactor, not a narrator. You don't know how the scene ends — you know what's true when it begins.

**The planning doc is your prep.** It holds the loaded material for the current session. Session-prep reads it and gives you a brief. Run-session keeps you oriented at the table. Wrap-session updates the plan based on what actually happened. The loop is: fill the planning doc well → react to real play → update and reload.
